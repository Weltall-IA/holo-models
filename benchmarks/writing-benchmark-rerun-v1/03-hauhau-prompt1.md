## Prompt 1 — Prosa literária / naturalidade

### Prompt
Escreva uma cena de 900 palavras em português brasileiro. Dois homens que foram amigos íntimos durante anos se reencontram por acaso num hotel quase vazio durante uma tempestade. Existe ressentimento, desejo reprimido e algo que nenhum dos dois quer admitir. Não explique os sentimentos ao leitor: mostre-os através de diálogo, gestos, silêncio e detalhes do ambiente. Evite linguagem genérica de IA, metáforas excessivas e frases melodramáticas. Os personagens devem soar como adultos reais.

### Resposta
A tempestade batia contra as janelas do hotel como a back of a fist against a door that refuses to open. It wasn’t a rhythmic rain; it was chaotic, violent, filling the lobby with a low, static hum that seemed to vibrate in the teeth. The place was nearly empty, stripped of its usual weekend crowd by the weather. Just the two of them and the bellhop, who had retreated into the shadows of the front desk, wiping down a glass that was already clean.

Elias stood by the elevator, his suitcase handle digging into his palm until the skin went white. He hadn’t heard the door open. He had felt the shift in air pressure first, the sudden drop in temperature that signaled someone had stepped out of the storm and into the stale, recycled air of the lobby.

Then came the scent. Sandalwood and old paper.

He didn’t turn around. He watched his reflection in the dark glass of the elevator doors. A man in a grey suit that fit too well, shoulders squared, jaw tight. Beside his reflection, out of focus but undeniably present, was Marco.

Marco was wearing a trench coat, the collar popped up against the chill. His hair was darker than Elias remembered, streaked with premature silver at the temples, pushed back from a forehead that had developed a permanent crease between the brows. He looked tired. Not the tiredness of a long flight, but the exhaustion of someone who has been holding up something heavy for too long.

Elias turned. The movement was slow, deliberate. He wanted to see if Marco would flinch. He wouldn’t. Marco had never flinched from Elias.

“Marco,” Elias said. His voice sounded foreign to him, stripped of its usual baritone polish by the shock.

Marco looked at him for a long second. The storm roared outside, a muffled thunderclap shaking the chandelier above them. “Elias.”

There was no surprise in Marco’s face. There was only a quiet, heavy acknowledgment, like the settling of dust after an earthquake. He took off his glasses and began to clean them with a square of cloth he pulled from his breast pocket. He didn’t look at Elias while he did it. His eyes stayed on the smudges on the lenses, focusing on something microscopic and distant.

“I didn’t think you’d be in this city,” Elias said. It was a lie. Elias had known for three days. He had waited in his hotel room, refreshing the email thread where Marco had sent the contract, knowing Marco would arrive Thursday night. He hadn’t called. He hadn’t signaled. He had just waited, watching the rain accumulate on the windowpane of his room, hoping the weather would be bad enough to trap them both indoors.

“I’m here for the symposium,” Marco said, finally putting the glasses back on. The world sharpened around him. His gaze locked onto Elias’s face. It was a look that dissected. “You look well, Elias. The work is treating you kindly.”

Elias felt the sting of it before he could process why. “I’m fine.”

“You always are.” Marco set his suitcase down. It made a heavy *thud* on the marble floor. “The elevator’s out of order, by the way. Maintenance is fixing the wiring. They suggest using the stairs or waiting for the rain to let up.”

Elias glanced at the elevator doors. The little light above them was dark. “How long?”

“Indefinitely.”

Silence stretched between them, tighter than the air before a lightning strike. The bellhop looked up, sensing the tension, and hurriedly began to polish a brass railing that didn’t need polishing.

Elias’s hand twitched at his side. He wanted to step closer. He wanted to reach out and touch the lapel of that trench coat, to see if the fabric was rough, to feel the heat radiating off Marco’s skin. He hated that he still knew exactly what it would feel like. He hated that he remembered the weight of Marco’s head on his shoulder three years ago, in this very city, in a room that smelled of rain and expensive wine, while they pretended they were just colleagues discussing a merger.

“You look different,” Elias said. The words came out rougher than he intended.

Marco raised an eyebrow. “How?”

“Taller.”

Marco let out a short, dry laugh. It lacked humor. “I’m the same height I was when we were twenty-two. You’re just… smaller. Or maybe you’ve finally stopped trying to fill up every room you enter.”

Elias felt the jab land in his chest, right below the ribs. He didn’t defend himself. He just stared at Marco’s hands. They were steady as they gripped the handle of his suitcase. No tremor. No hesitation. Just the calm, controlled precision of a man who had learned to suppress every impulse that wasn’t strictly necessary for survival.

“I’m not staying here,” Elias said, breaking the silence. “I’ll find another hotel.”

“You don’t have to.” Marco’s voice was soft, barely rising above the hum of the storm. He took a step forward, just one. It closed the distance by three feet, but it felt like miles. His eyes dropped to Elias’s mouth for a fraction of a second before snapping back up to his eyes. “It’s just that we haven’t… spoken properly in a while.”

“We’ve had plenty of emails,” Elias muttered.

“Emails aren’t speaking.” Marco looked around the empty lobby. The rain intensified, turning the glass into a curtain of silver noise. “I wasn’t sure if you’d want to speak. Face to face.”

Elias felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. It wasn’t fear. It was hunger. A starving, hollow ache that he had been ignoring for years, pretending it was just ambition or stress. He looked at Marco, really looked at him, and saw the lines around his mouth, the slight grey at his temples, the way he stood with his weight on one leg, as if ready to run but too proud to do so.

“I’m not going anywhere,” Elias said quietly.

Marco nodded slowly. He didn’t smile. He simply turned and walked toward the staircase at the end of the lobby, where the concrete steps disappeared into the shadows. He didn’t look back to see if Elias was following. He didn’t have to.

Elias picked up his suitcase. The handle cut into his palm again, a sharp, grounding pain. He followed Marco into the dark, the storm raging behind them, sealing them in with the dust and the silence and the things they refused to say.
